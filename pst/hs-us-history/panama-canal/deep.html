Phase 2 — Connect & organize

Now students take the facts from Phase 1 and relate them: mapping benefits against costs, interrogating real primary sources, and building an argument from evidence. Deep learning is where knowledge becomes usable — the bridge to the problem.

🎯 By the end of Phase 2 students can organize the benefits and costs of building the canal, read a primary source for its point of view and reliability, and defend an evaluative claim about expansionism with evidence — the exact moves the problem will demand.
🎟️ Entry ticket — 1-minute warm-up
Concept mapping · d 0.64§113.41(c)(12), (c)(4)(B)

1 · Benefits–costs concept map

Build a class concept map with "Build & control the canal" in the center. On the left, cluster the benefits the U.S. saw; on the right, the costs — to Colombia, to Panama, and to the workers. Then add a third ring: long-term effects — what followed because the U.S. acted as it did in 1903.

faster Atlantic–Pacific naval movementColombia loses a province and the canalU.S. emerges as a world power

Talk move: draw an arrow from any benefit or cost to a long-term effect and say the cause-and-effect sentence aloud. This rehearses §113.41(c)(12) and sets up the evaluation in (c)(4)(B).

Elaboration & organization · d 0.72§113.41(c)(28)(A,B,C)

2 · Primary-source analysis — read for point of view

Give pairs one or two real (district-approved) primary sources — for example Roosevelt's boast "I took the Canal Zone," the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty's "in perpetuity" clause, a "big stick" political cartoon, or photographs of construction and of the West Indian labor force. Use a four-question source routine:

  1. Source: Who made this, when, and why?
  2. Observe: What do you actually see or read? (facts only)
  3. Point of view: Whose story does it tell — and whose is missing?
  4. Question: What does it make you want to find out?

Corroboration check (c)(28)(B): is this a first-hand record or a later opinion? What other source would confirm or complicate it? How do we know?

⚠️ Handling difficult history: the segregated "gold roll / silver roll" system paid and housed white American workers far better than the Black West Indian laborers who did the deadliest work. Study it honestly and respectfully as documented fact — the point is understanding, not blame or sensationalism (TEC §28.0022).

Argumentation · d 0.86§113.41(c)(4)(B), (c)(28)(E), (c)(29)

3 · Structured argument — a warm-up claim

A low-stakes rehearsal of the reasoning the problem needs. Pose a focused evaluative question and have students take a side with evidence, using a claim–evidence–reasoning frame. Then have them state the other side's strongest point (civil discourse) — this is the evaluate-expansionism move of §113.41(c)(4)(B).

Warm-up question: Was U.S. expansionism in 1903 a justified pursuit of national interest, or an overreach against another nation's sovereignty? Give your claim, one piece of evidence from Phase 1 or the sources, and your reasoning.

Sentence stems (ELPS support): "My claim is ______." · "My evidence is ______." · "This matters because ______." · "Someone who disagrees might say ______, but ______."

Note: keep this a practice argument about the general question. The specific 1903 decision — with stakeholders in the room — belongs in Phase 3.

✅ Deep check before the problem: can students name a benefit and a cost of the canal, read one source for its point of view and reliability, and state an evaluative claim with evidence? Those three abilities are exactly what Phase 3 will ask them to transfer.
