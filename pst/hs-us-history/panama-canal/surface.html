Phase 1 — Build the knowledge

Before students can reason about the canal problem, they need the raw material: the vocabulary, the strategic facts, and the geography. These three activities are fast and front-loaded — the goal is acquisition, not yet analysis.

🎯 By the end of Phase 1 students can use the key vocabulary, explain why a canal mattered for trade and naval power, summarize the French failure and the Panama-vs-Nicaragua route debate, and locate the isthmus and the world's major shipping lanes on a map.

Jigsaw method · d 0.92§113.41(c)(4)(A), (c)(28)(A)

2 · Jigsaw reading — Why a canal, and why Panama?

Split the class into four expert groups, each studying one topic below, then re-mix into home groups where every topic is represented. Each expert teaches their group. (Jigsaw is one of the highest-leverage surface moves precisely because students must teach.)

🧩 Use the ACE Powered Jigsaw Organizer — experts capture their notes on it before teaching their home group: open the organizer ↗. New to running a jigsaw? See the teacher guides in the facilitator guide.

Sources for each expert group (free, reputable; confirm access through your district — links open in a new tab):

A · Sea power & the strategic case (Mahan)

Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that national greatness rested on sea power. A canal would let the U.S. Navy shift between the Atlantic and Pacific quickly — the lesson of the battleship Oregon's 66-day voyage around South America in 1898.

📄 Office of the Historian · Panama Canal ↗
📄 Student Handouts · The Panama Canal & the Americas ↗

B · The French failure under de Lesseps

Ferdinand de Lesseps, builder of the Suez Canal, launched a French effort in the 1880s. Yellow fever, malaria, landslides, and money troubles killed roughly 22,000 workers and bankrupted the company before it collapsed.

📄 PBS · American Experience: The French Attempt ↗
📄 Office of the Historian · Panama Canal ↗

C · Panama vs. Nicaragua — the route debate

Congress nearly chose a longer, sea-level Nicaragua route. Lobbyist Philippe Bunau-Varilla swayed the vote toward Panama — partly with a Nicaraguan postage stamp showing an erupting volcano to warn of danger.

📄 Library of Congress · History of the Panama Canal ↗
📄 PBS · Nicaragua or Panama? ↗

D · Conquering disease & building the locks

Dr. William Gorgas's mosquito-control campaign made the American effort possible; chief engineer George Goethals designed a high, lock-and-lake canal. It opened in 1914 — but at a cost of about 5,600 lives in the U.S. era.

📄 PBS · American Experience: Disease & the Canal ↗
📄 Library of Congress · History of the Panama Canal ↗

Check for understanding: each home group writes two sentences answering "Give one reason a canal mattered to U.S. power, and one reason building it was so hard."

Direct instruction · d 0.56§113.41(c)(12)

3 · Map work — the isthmus, the two routes, and the world's shipping lanes

Using a world map, students locate the Isthmus of Panama and mark both candidate routes: Panama (shorter, mountainous, lock canal) and Nicaragua (longer, using Lake Nicaragua, near active volcanoes). Then trace the pre-canal voyage around Cape Horn and the world shipping lanes a canal would shorten — New York ↔ San Francisco dropping from ~13,000 to ~5,000 nautical miles.

Quick write: "A canal at ______ would shorten the trip from ______ to ______, which matters for ______ (trade / naval power) because ______."
