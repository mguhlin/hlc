Phase 1 — Build the knowledge

Before students can reason about the missile crisis, they need the raw material: the vocabulary, the strategic facts, and the geography. These three activities are fast and front-loaded — the goal is acquisition, not yet analysis.

🎯 By the end of Phase 1 students can use the key vocabulary, explain what the U-2 photos revealed and why the missiles alarmed Washington, summarize the players and the thirteen-day timeline, and locate Cuba and the missiles' range on a map.
🎟️ Entry ticket — 1-minute warm-up
Vocabulary & feedback · d 0.62§113.41(c)(8)(A), (c)(8)(B)

1 · Word bank & vocabulary sort

Introduce and let students sort the unit vocabulary. Sort twice: first by "words about weapons & strategy" vs. "words about diplomacy & power," then by student-invented categories (feedback on their reasoning).

TermWorking definition
Cold Warthe decades-long rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, fought through threats, proxies, and arms rather than direct combat
containmentthe U.S. policy of stopping the spread of communism rather than rolling it back
deterrencepreventing an enemy's attack by convincing them the cost of attacking would be too high
brinkmanshippushing a dangerous confrontation to the edge of war to force the other side to back down
blockade / "quarantine"a naval ring around Cuba to stop Soviet ships; Kennedy called it a quarantine because a blockade is legally an act of war
ICBMan intercontinental ballistic missile — a rocket that can carry a nuclear warhead across continents
ExCommthe Executive Committee of the National Security Council — Kennedy's small crisis advisory group
MAD (mutually assured destruction)the idea that if either superpower launched a nuclear attack, both would be destroyed — so neither dares to start
sphere of influencea region where one nation holds dominant political or military power over others
stakeholderanyone who is affected by a decision or has something at stake in it

Jigsaw method · d 0.92§113.41(c)(8)(A), (c)(28)

2 · Jigsaw reading — How did thirteen days bring the world to the brink?

Split the class into four expert groups, each studying one topic below, then re-mix into home groups where every topic is represented. Each expert teaches their group. (Jigsaw is one of the highest-leverage surface moves precisely because students must teach.)

Sources for each expert group (free, reputable; confirm access through your district — links open in a new tab):

A · The Cold War backdrop & the Bay of Pigs

By 1962 the U.S. and U.S.S.R. were locked in an arms race and a contest for influence. In 1961 a U.S.-backed invasion of Cuba at the Bay of Pigs failed, pushing Fidel Castro closer to Moscow and making a Soviet defense of Cuba plausible.

📄 Office of the Historian · Cuban Missile Crisis ↗
📄 JFK Library · The Bay of Pigs ↗

B · The U-2 photos & the discovery

On October 14, 1962, a U-2 spy plane photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba. Analysts confirmed the missiles could strike much of the United States, and Kennedy convened ExComm in secret.

📄 JFK Library · The Cuban Missile Crisis ↗
📄 National Security Archive · Key Moments ↗

C · The thirteen days & the players

For thirteen days — October 16 to 28 — President Kennedy, Soviet Premier Khrushchev, and Cuban leader Castro maneuvered. Kennedy chose a naval "quarantine" over an airstrike; the world watched as Soviet ships approached the line.

📄 JFK Library · Cuban Missile Crisis ↗
📄 Office of the Historian · Cuban Missile Crisis ↗

D · The resolution & the Jupiter missiles

The crisis ended on October 28 when Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles. Publicly, the U.S. pledged not to invade Cuba; secretly, it also agreed to remove its own Jupiter missiles from Turkey and Italy months later.

Check for understanding: each home group writes two sentences answering "Give one reason the missiles alarmed the U.S., and one reason a peaceful resolution was so hard to reach."

Direct instruction · d 0.56§113.41(c)(8)(B)

3 · Map work — Cuba, missile range, and the two superpowers

Using a world map, students locate Cuba — about 90 miles from Florida — and draw the range rings of the Soviet missiles based there: the medium-range missiles (~1,000 mi) reaching Washington, D.C., and the intermediate-range missiles (~2,000 mi) reaching most of the continental United States. Then mark the U.S. Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, sitting the same short distance from Soviet cities.

Quick write: "A missile in ______ could reach ______ in minutes, which matters for ______ (deterrence / security) because ______."
