High School — U.S. History Since 1877

Problem-Based Learning units for the high-school U.S. history course (§113.41) — also suited to AP U.S. History, dual-credit, and early-college. Each unit moves students from building knowledge (surface) to organizing it (deep) to solving a real, ill-structured problem as a stakeholder (transfer).

① Surface

Build the knowledge

Vocabulary, key facts, jigsaw reading, map & source work.

② Deep

Connect & organize

Concept maps, primary sources, points of view, argument.

③ Transfer

Solve the problem

Meet the problem, take a role, investigate, propose a solution, debrief.

Featured · U.S. History · §113.41

The Panama Canal, 1903 — Should the U.S. Build and Control It?

It is 1903. Students become stakeholders — President Roosevelt, a Colombian official, a Panamanian nationalist, a West Indian laborer, a naval-power advocate — and work the question of whether, how, and on whose terms the United States should build a canal across the isthmus. Imperialism, sovereignty, and the human cost of a world power's ambition. 5–8 class periods.

The Progressive Era — Should We Regulate Big Business?

It is 1906 and industrial America is booming — and breaking. Students become a muckraker, a factory owner, a labor organizer, a reformer, an immigrant worker, and a worried consumer, and weigh whether — and how far — government should regulate business and protect people. 5–8 class periods.

The New Deal, 1935 — a Question of Government's Reach

The Depression grinds on. Students become FDR, an unemployed worker, a business owner, a Supreme Court justice, a Black sharecropper, and a critic who says it doesn't go far enough — and decide how much responsibility the federal government should take for the economy and its citizens. 5–8 class periods.

The Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962 — Thirteen Days on the Brink

October 1962: Soviet nuclear missiles are 90 miles from Florida. Students become Kennedy, a hawkish general, a diplomat, RFK, and Soviet and Cuban voices, and work the hardest decision of the Cold War — how to respond without ending the world. 5–8 class periods.

Birmingham, 1963 — the Civil Rights Movement

Spring 1963 in Birmingham. When the law itself is unjust, how should the movement push for change? Students become Dr. King, a student activist, a more militant voice, a white moderate, a segregationist official, and a federal official — and weigh strategy, risk, and justice. 5–8 class periods.
