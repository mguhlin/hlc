The New Deal, 1935 — a Question of Government's Reach

A Problem-Based Learning unit. Students step into the Depression year of 1935, take on a stakeholder's point of view, and work a real, ill-structured question about government, the economy, and citizens' welfare — building from surface to deep to transfer learning. The teacher is a guide, not the answer key.

Driving question: It is 1935 and the Depression grinds on. Should the federal government take on this much responsibility for the economy and its citizens — and at what cost to other principles?

The three-phase path (do them in order)

Problem-solving is a transfer move — it only works once students have knowledge to reason with. So the problem in Phase 3 is deliberately gated behind Phases 1 and 2.

① Surface

Build the knowledge

Vocabulary, key facts about the crash, the Depression, and the alphabet agencies, a jigsaw read, and data work on unemployment. ~1–2 periods.

 ② Deep

Connect & organize

Benefits–costs and relief–recovery–reform concept map, primary sources, competing points of view, and a structured argument. ~1–2 periods.

 ③ Transfer

Solve the problem

Meet the problem, take a stakeholder role, investigate, propose & defend a New-Deal stance, debrief. ~2–4 periods.
⛔ Gate: don't open the Phase 3 problem until students have finished the surface and deep activities. Meeting the problem too early turns inquiry into guessing.

Big idea & objectives

Big idea: In a crisis, a nation must weigh economic security, individual liberty, constitutional limits, and cost when it decides how much responsibility government should take — and the choices made in the New Deal permanently expanded the federal role while leaving a contested legacy of debt, power, and unequal benefit.

  • Know: what caused the Great Depression, what the New Deal's relief, recovery, and reform programs actually did, and how the New Deal reshaped the roles of state and federal government.
  • Understand: the same New Deal looks profoundly different from each stakeholder's point of view — a New Dealer, an unemployed worker, a business owner, a Supreme Court justice, a Black sharecropper, and a critic who says it does not go far enough.
  • Do: run the problem-solving and decision-making process end to end — identify the problem, gather information, list and weigh options, choose and defend a solution, and evaluate it (§113.41(c)(31)(B)).

Teacher prep & materials

  • Print the facilitator guide (teacher moves, map of possibilities, role cards, debrief prompts) and the assessment pack (rubric + individual-in-group).
  • Chart paper or a projected space for three shared columns: Hunches · Know · Need-to-Know (the KWHL chart).
  • Vetted sources for investigation (district-approved). Suggested public-access sources are listed in the facilitator guide.
  • No logins, no accounts — every page runs in the browser. Student writing and products live on paper or in tools your district already vets.
⚠️ Honest history: this unit teaches the New Deal's real exclusions — Social Security originally left out agricultural and domestic workers (disproportionately Black and women), and relief was often administered unequally — directly, factually, and respectfully, alongside its genuine relief and lasting reforms and the constitutional fight over federal power (TEC §28.0022).
