Phase 2 — Connect & organize

Now students take the facts from Phase 1 and relate them: mapping problems to the reforms that answered them, interrogating real primary sources, and building an argument from evidence. Deep learning is where knowledge becomes usable — the bridge to the problem.

🎯 By the end of Phase 2 students can connect each problem of industrialization to a reform, read a primary source for its point of view and reliability, and defend an evaluative claim about regulation with evidence — the exact moves the problem will demand.
🎟️ Entry ticket — 1-minute warm-up
Concept mapping · d 0.64§113.41(c)(5), (c)(5)(A)

1 · Problems → reforms cause & effect map

Build a class concept map with "Industrial America, 1906" in the center. On the left, cluster the problems industrialization created; on the right, the reforms that responded. Then draw arrows connecting each problem to its reform, and add a third ring: long-term effects — what followed because Progressives acted.

ProblemReform that respondedLong-term effect
unsafe food & adulterated medicinePure Food and Drug Act & Meat Inspection Act (1906)federal consumer protection; later the FDA
monopolies & trusts fixing pricestrust-busting; regulation of railroads & industrya permanent regulatory role for government
party bosses & corrupt machinesinitiative, referendum, recall; direct primary; 17th Amendmentmore direct democracy — but access left uneven

Talk move: draw an arrow from any problem to a reform to a long-term effect and say the cause-and-effect sentence aloud. This rehearses §113.41(c)(5) and sets up the analysis in (c)(5)(A).

Elaboration & organization · d 0.72§113.41(c)(28)

2 · Primary-source analysis — read for point of view

Give pairs one or two real (district-approved) primary sources — for example an excerpt from Upton Sinclair's The Jungle, a Tarbell exposé of Standard Oil or a Jacob Riis tenement photograph, a political cartoon on the trusts, or the text of the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906). Use a four-question source routine:

  1. Source: Who made this, when, and why?
  2. Observe: What do you actually see or read? (facts only)
  3. Point of view: Whose story does it tell — and whose is missing?
  4. Question: What does it make you want to find out?

Corroboration check (c)(28): is this a first-hand record or a later opinion? What other source would confirm or complicate it? How do we know?

⚠️ Handling difficult history: the Progressive movement won real gains — food safety, direct democracy, suffrage — yet many reforms excluded or harmed Black Americans, segregation deepened in this era, and some Progressives embraced eugenics and immigration restriction. Study this honestly and respectfully as documented fact — the point is understanding, not blame or sensationalism (TEC §28.0022).

Argumentation · d 0.86§113.41(c)(5)(A), (c)(28), (c)(29)

3 · Structured argument — a warm-up claim

A low-stakes rehearsal of the reasoning the problem needs. Pose a focused evaluative question and have students take a side with evidence, using a claim–evidence–reasoning frame. Then have them state the other side's strongest point (civil discourse) — this rehearses the reform-impact analysis of §113.41(c)(5)(A).

Warm-up question: Was Progressive regulation of business a necessary protection of the public, or an overreach against economic freedom? Give your claim, one piece of evidence from Phase 1 or the sources, and your reasoning.

Sentence stems (ELPS support): "My claim is ______." · "My evidence is ______." · "This matters because ______." · "Someone who disagrees might say ______, but ______."

Note: keep this a practice argument about the general question. The specific 1906 decision — with stakeholders in the room — belongs in Phase 3.

✅ Deep check before the problem: can students connect a problem to the reform that answered it, read one source for its point of view and reliability, and state an evaluative claim with evidence? Those three abilities are exactly what Phase 3 will ask them to transfer.
