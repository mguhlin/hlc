Birmingham, 1963 — the Civil Rights Movement

A Problem-Based Learning unit. Students step into the spring of 1963 in Birmingham, take on a stakeholder's point of view, and work a real, ill-structured question about unjust law, nonviolence, and change — building from surface to deep to transfer learning. The teacher is a guide, not the answer key.

Driving question: It is the spring of 1963 in Birmingham. When the law itself is unjust, how should the movement push for change — and how should students, leaders, and officials respond?

The three-phase path (do them in order)

Problem-solving is a transfer move — it only works once students have knowledge to reason with. So the problem in Phase 3 is deliberately gated behind Phases 1 and 2.

① Surface

Build the knowledge

Vocabulary, key facts about segregation and the movement, a jigsaw read, and a timeline of the road to Birmingham. ~1–2 periods.

 ② Deep

Connect & organize

Strategies–consequences concept map, primary sources, competing points of view, and a structured argument. ~1–2 periods.

 ③ Transfer

Solve the problem

Meet the problem, take a stakeholder role, investigate, propose & defend a course of action, debrief. ~2–4 periods.
⛔ Gate: don't open the Phase 3 problem until students have finished the surface and deep activities. Meeting the problem too early turns inquiry into guessing.

Big idea & objectives

Big idea: When people conclude that the law itself is unjust, they must weigh strategy, risk, and conscience in deciding how to press for change — and the choices made in Birmingham in 1963 moved a nation and shaped the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

  • Know: the development of the civil rights movement — Brown v. Board, the Montgomery Bus Boycott, the sit-ins and SNCC, the Birmingham Campaign and Children's Crusade, the March on Washington, and the landmark laws that resulted.
  • Understand: the same spring of 1963 looks profoundly different from each stakeholder's point of view — leader, student, moderate, official, and federal government alike.
  • Do: run the problem-solving and decision-making process end to end — identify the problem, gather information, list and weigh options, choose and defend a course of action, and evaluate it (§113.41(c)(31)(B)).

Teacher prep & materials

  • Print the facilitator guide (teacher moves, map of possibilities, role cards, debrief prompts) and the assessment pack (rubric + individual-in-group).
  • Chart paper or a projected space for three shared columns: Hunches · Know · Need-to-Know (the KWHL chart).
  • Vetted sources for investigation (district-approved). Suggested public-access sources are listed in the facilitator guide.
  • No logins, no accounts — every page runs in the browser. Student writing and products live on paper or in tools your district already vets.
⚠️ Honest history: this unit teaches the violence used against nonviolent protesters — police dogs, fire hoses, mass arrests, and the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing that killed four girls — directly, factually, and with dignity (TEC §28.0022). The segregationist official's role is presented as a documented historical position, not an endorsement.
