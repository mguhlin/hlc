Facilitator Guide

How to run Birmingham, 1963 as Problem-Based Learning: your role, the pre-planning maps, pacing, role cards, sources, and debrief prompts. The golden rule — guide, don't tell. In PBL the students should feel they, not you, planned the investigation.

Your role by phase

PhaseWhat you doWhat you resist
① SurfaceTeach vocabulary and key facts of the movement efficiently; run the jigsaw; check acquisition.Rushing to the problem before facts are secure.
② DeepFacilitate the strategies–consequences map and source routine; model claim + evidence.Giving your own verdict on the strategies or the sources.
③ TransferRead the problem well; hold the KWHL chart; answer questions with questions; point to sources.Answering the "Need to know" questions yourself.

Running the jigsaw (Phase 1)

The Surface phase uses a four-topic jigsaw (How did the movement reach Birmingham?) — a high-leverage move (d ≈ 0.92) because every student must teach. The flow: expert groups each study one topic and take notes → students re-mix into home groups with one expert per topic → each expert teaches → an individual check for understanding holds everyone accountable.

Pre-planning · Map of Possibilities

Before teaching, brainstorm every direction the Birmingham problem could branch — so you can steer discussion and decide, in advance, which threads are productive and which are too sensitive or off-topic for your class and community.

Center: Birmingham, 1963. Branches: nonviolent direct action · civil disobedience & the law · the Children's Crusade · the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" · the moderate "wait" position · the segregationist order · federal intervention · the 1964 & 1965 laws. Mark threads you will encourage and any you will redirect (keep it factual, professional, and age-appropriate — relevant, not inflammatory).

Suggested pacing (5–8 class periods)

  • Period 1: Surface — vocabulary sort + jigsaw.
  • Period 2: Surface timeline work → Deep strategies–consequences map.
  • Period 3: Deep — primary sources + warm-up argument.
  • Period 4: Transfer — Meet the Problem, roles, Hunches/Know/Need-to-Know.
  • Periods 5–6: Transfer — inquiry & investigation.
  • Periods 7–8: Transfer — build, present, and debrief the 8-part brief.

Facilitation prompts (keep these handy)

  • "What in the record makes you say that?"
  • "Where could we find that out? Which source?"
  • "How do you know — is that a fact or a guess?"
  • "Whose point of view are we missing — a student's, a moderate's, a federal official's?"
  • "What are the advantages and disadvantages of that option?"
  • "How would you know if the course of action actually worked — then and now?"

Suggested public-access / vetted sources

Confirm access through your district's approved catalog before class. Vetted, free starting points:

  • Overview & place: National Park Service · Birmingham Civil Rights National Monument ↗
  • Photographs, oral histories & documents: Library of Congress · Civil Rights History Project ↗
  • Primary-source documents & analysis: National Archives · DocsTeach: Civil Rights ↗ · LoC · Getting Started with Primary Sources ↗
  • Key documents: King Institute (Stanford) · "Letter from Birmingham Jail" ↗ · NMAAHC · Stories from the movement ↗
  • The laws that followed: National Archives · The Civil Rights Act of 1964 ↗
  • The bombing & its cost: NPS · 16th Street Baptist Church Bombing (1963) ↗
⚠️ Keep sources grade-appropriate and community-appropriate. This unit teaches the violence used against nonviolent protesters — dogs, fire hoses, arrests, and the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing — and the segregationist position as history and as a problem-solving process: factual, relevant, and respectful, never inflammatory, and never an endorsement (TEC §28.0022). Students' outside research should use tools your district already vets.
