The painting gallery

Eleven American history paintings, each with the era or issue it opens and the standards it touches. Every image links to the museum or collection that holds it — none are reproduced here. Reveal the title only after the "Articulate It" observations.

🖼️ How to use this page: pick one painting for a 10–15 minute routine. Project it from the linked museum page (do not download or reproduce it), run Articulate → Connect → Extend, and collect the three-line exit ticket. Paintings marked ⚠️ handle with care carry bias or difficult history — teach the omission, not around it (TEC §28.0022: factual, respectful, non-inflammatory).
Revolutionary memory · leadership§113.41(c)(1), (c)(19)

Washington Crossing the Delaware — Emanuel Leutze, 1851

Painted 75 years after the event as heroic memory, not reportage. Great for point-of-view: what does the composition ask you to feel, and how does that differ from the cold, chaotic reality of 1776?

🔗 The Metropolitan Museum of Art · view the painting ↗

Founding documents · civic memory§113.41(c)(1); §113.44(c)

Declaration of Independence — John Trumbull, 1818

The Capitol Rotunda version. Trumbull staged a moment that never happened exactly this way. Who is present, who is absent, and how does a painting shape a nation's memory of its founding?

🔗 Architect of the Capitol · view the painting ↗

Constitutional republic · representative gov't§113.44(c); §113.41(c)(1)

Scene at the Signing of the Constitution — Howard Chandler Christy, 1940

A 20th-century image of an 18th-century event, hung in the Capitol. Pairs well with the Trumbull for a "how does art build civic memory?" comparison.

🔗 Architect of the Capitol · view the painting ↗

Westward expansion · Manifest Destiny§113.41(c)(2), (c)(30)

American Progress — after John Gast, 1872 ⚠️

⚠️ Handle with care. A promotional image of Manifest Destiny that literally pushes Native peoples and animals into the dark margins. Teach the bias directly: whose progress, and at whose cost? A model text for the "what does this leave out?" Extend move.

🔗 Library of Congress · the mass-market print ↗ · Autry Museum · the original 1872 painting ↗ · Charles Hilliard, Reversing Manifest Destiny (2021) ↗ — a Sokaogon Chippewa artist reverses Gast's whole composition. Compare the three: whose “progress,” moving which way, and what does each leave out?

Industrialization · urbanization · labor§113.41(c)(4), (c)(13)

America Today — Thomas Hart Benton, 1930–31

A mural cycle of 1920s industry, cities, and work. Rich for reading many scenes at once: what does Benton celebrate about modern America, and what tensions does he show?

🔗 The Metropolitan Museum of Art · view the murals ↗

Progressive Era · urban life · race & law§113.41(c)(4), (c)(5)

Both Members of This Club — George Bellows, 1909 ⚠️

⚠️ Handle with care. A brutal boxing scene from the era of illegal club fights — and a rare depiction of a Black and a white fighter. Opens hard questions about race, class, spectacle, and the law in Progressive-Era cities. Preview before teaching.

🔗 National Gallery of Art · view the painting ↗

Red Scare · nativism · civil liberties§113.41(c)(6), (c)(21)

The Passion of Sacco and Vanzetti — Ben Shahn, 1931–32 ⚠️

⚠️ Handle with care. Shahn's protest image of two Italian immigrants executed after a contested trial. A study in how art makes an argument — and a way into 1920s nativism, immigration, and civil liberties. It is a point of view, not a neutral record.

🔗 Whitney Museum of American Art · view the painting ↗

Great Migration · WWI home front · labor§113.41(c)(4), (c)(7)

The Migration Series, Panel 1 — Jacob Lawrence, 1940–41

"During World War I there was a great migration north by southern African Americans." Panel 1 of 60. Lawrence tells the Great Migration as the migrants' own story — a corrective to images that erase them. Consider the whole series as a visual argument.

🔗 The Phillips Collection · view the series ↗

WWII · women in the workforce · propaganda§113.41(c)(8), (c)(30)

Rosie the Riveter — Norman Rockwell, 1943

A Saturday Evening Post cover of a muscular woman war worker (posed after Michelangelo's Isaiah). Great for reading wartime messaging: who is this image meant to persuade, and to do what?

🔗 Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art · view the painting ↗

Civil rights · desegregation · Brown v. Board§113.41(c)(9), (c)(24)

The Problem We All Live With — Norman Rockwell, 1964 ⚠️

⚠️ Handle with care. Six-year-old Ruby Bridges walking into a desegregated New Orleans school, flanked by marshals, a slur on the wall behind her. The strongest "run it tomorrow" opener. Teach the history honestly and respectfully (TEC §28.0022).

🔗 Norman Rockwell Museum · view the painting ↗

Space Race · Apollo · Cold War science§113.41(c)(10), (c)(27)

Man's First Step on the Moon — Norman Rockwell, 1966

Rockwell reconstructed the lunar landing three years before it happened, from NASA specs. A way into the Space Race, Cold War competition, and how science and national identity intertwined.

🔗 Smithsonian · view the painting ↗
